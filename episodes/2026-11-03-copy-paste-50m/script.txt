One copy and paste cost a crypto trader almost fifty million dollars. Nobody can undo it.
Last December, that trader moved fifty million digital dollars, called USDT, from Binance, the crypto exchange, to their own wallet.
First, a fifty-dollar test to the right address, the code you send money to.
Here's the trick. Within minutes, a scammer made a fake address that starts and ends like the real one, and sent the trader half a cent from it. Now the fake sat in the wallet's history.
Twenty-six minutes after the test, the trader copied the address from that history to send the rest. The fake one. Almost fifty million dollars, gone.
Tether, which issues USDT, can freeze stolen coins. So within half an hour, the scammer swapped it all into a coin nobody can freeze, then hid the trail in a mixer.
The trader offered a million-dollar reward for the money. Nothing came back.
The trader's two mistakes: copying from the history, and not reading the whole address. Save checked addresses as contacts, so you never copy from history. And read the middle: fakes only copy the ends.
