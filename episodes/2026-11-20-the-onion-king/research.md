## Facts used (every on-screen and spoken claim)

1. **Who.** Vincent W. Kosuga, of Pine Island, New York: onion grower and shipper, trader, member of the Chicago Mercantile
   Exchange. Sam S. Siegel: exchange member, president and main shareholder of National Produce Distributors, a wholesale
   produce firm in Franklin Park, Illinois. (CEA decision, respondents.) NPR Planet Money, "The Great Onion Corner And The
   Futures Market" (Keith Romer, Oct 2015): Kosuga "was an onion farmer in upstate New York"; "business partner, Sam Siegel".
   https://www.npr.org/transcripts/450769853
   → Script: "The men behind it: Vincent Kosuga and Sam Siegel." Tag: "Kosuga & Siegel".
2. **Nearly every onion in Chicago, about thirty million pounds, late 1955.** CEA decision, Finding 5: in early December 1955
   Kosuga had 321 carlots in Chicago cold storage and on track and about 400 more in country storage, National 250: about 1,000
   carlots, which "constituted practically the total supply of onions in Chicago which were available and could qualify for
   delivery on the Chicago Mercantile Exchange." Finding 15: on 31 Dec 1955 their 556 carlots in Chicago cold storage were
   "98 percent of all cold storage stocks in Chicago". Finding 3: a carlot is "600 50-pound bags, more or less", i.e. about
   30,000 lb, so 1,000 carlots ≈ 30 million lb. The same figure in Greising & Morse, *Brokers, Bagmen, and Moles* (Wiley, 1991),
   p. 81, as cited by Wikipedia "Onion Futures Act": "they had stored 30 million pounds (14,000 t) of onions in Chicago."
   https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Onion_Futures_Act
   → Script: "You own nearly every onion in the city". Tag (not spoken): "30,000,000 lb".
   Note: two men and a firm held them, not one; "you" is the cartoon. Not all 1,000 carlots sat inside Chicago (400 were in
   country storage), so the script says "thirty million pounds" and "nearly every onion in the city" as two separate facts.
3. **Eight pounds for every person in Chicago (the narrator's sum).** Chicago's population in the 1950 census: 3,620,962
   (https://www.biggestuscities.com/il/1950; the Encyclopedia of Chicago gives 3,621,092:
   https://encyclopedia.chicagohistory.org/pages/962.html). 30,000,000 ÷ 3.62 million = 8.3 lb.
   → Script: "Eight pounds of onion for every person in Chicago. Including the babies." Tag: "8 lb each".
4. **They had started to rot.** CEA decision, Finding 15: "most of the onions which he owned in cold storage were decayed and
   would not qualify for delivery"; p. 13: "the bulk of the onions owned by respondents in Chicago cold storage had
   deteriorated"; Finding 16: carlots were shipped to Davenport, Iowa for "eliminating spoilage and rebagging".
   → Script: "they're rotting."
5. **The growers bought some back on a promise that the rest stayed off the market.** CEA decision, Finding 7: at meetings
   about 27 Nov, 10 Dec and 17 Dec 1955, grower-shippers agreed to buy "287 carlots" at "$ 1.60 per bag or $ 960 per carlot,
   for a total sale price of $ 275,520", and Kosuga committed to "make no deliveries on any exchange" for the rest of the
   season; Finding 8: the respondents received "approximately $ 175,000"; Finding 9: the purpose was "strengthening and
   supporting the market". Time, "Commodities: Odorous Onions", 2 July 1956 (as cited by Wikipedia): they "told the growers
   that they would hold the rest of their inventory in order to support the price of onions."
   → Script: "Dump them, and the price collapses. So farmers buy some, if you lock up the rest." / "You've sold onions by
   promising not to sell onions." (the narrator's summary of Findings 7 and 9)
6. **Then they bet on the price falling, and the onions came onto the market.** CEA decision, Finding 13: from 27 Dec 1955 to
   2 Feb 1956 they raised their combined short position in the March 1956 onion future from 303 carlots to 1,161 (44.3% of all
   open contracts); Finding 18: in March they delivered 108 of the 171 initial carlots delivered. NPR: "there's a way in the
   futures market to profit off of that, to make a bet about prices falling"; "in March of 1956... All those onions he'd been
   hoarding, he flooded the market with them."
   → Script: "You sell futures: promises to deliver onions later, at today's price. If onions get cheaper, you keep the
   difference. So you unlock the onions." (a futures contract explained in one clause; "short" is not used. "Unlock": Finding 7's
   promise of no deliveries for the season, against Finding 18's 108 carlots delivered in March 1956)
7. **March 1956: ten cents for a fifty-pound bag.** CEA decision, Finding 20: the March 1956 future, "on March 15, the last
   trading day, it reached a low of ten cents and closed at 15 cents"; p. 15: from $1.16 on 15 Feb, "a decline of 91 percent".
   The contract was priced per 50-lb bag (Finding 3: 600 50-pound bags a carlot; Finding 7: "$1.60 per bag or $960 per carlot").
   NPR (Harvey Paffenroth, Kosuga's nephew): "A 50-pound bag of onions, they went to 10 cents a bag." Greising & Morse p. 81 (via
   Wikipedia): "driven the price of 50 pounds (23 kg) of onions down to 10 cents a bag."
   → Script: "March 1956. A fifty-pound bag: ten cents." Tags: "March 1956", "10¢ a bag".
8. **The empty bag is said to have cost more.** NPR (Paffenroth): "the bag alone empty costs 20 cents." Lambert, *The Futures*
   (Basic Books, 2010), p. 42 (via Wikipedia): onions "were selling in Chicago for less than the bags that held them." The
   government record does not give a bag price, so the script says "the story goes".
   → Script: "The story goes the empty bag cost twenty." / "Tip the onions out, and you've doubled your money." (the narrator's
   sum: 10¢ full, 20¢ empty). Tag: "empty bag: 20¢".
9. **The government ruled they manipulated the price.** CEA decision (USDA Judicial Officer, 3 June 1960), syllabus:
   respondents "attempted to manipulate and to have manipulated downward" the March 1956 onion future and cash onion prices;
   Finding 20: "artificial prices owing to the activities of respondents". The charge that Kosuga manipulated prices upward in
   November 1955 was dismissed. Order: Kosuga's floor-broker registration revoked; all three barred from trading for ten months.
   The decision adds that "the Texas onion crop had some depressing effect" too, and that it is enough "that respondents'
   action contributed to the price decline."
   → Script: "The government ruled they'd manipulated the price." (the record's own verb; no "caused the crash alone")
10. **In 1958 Congress banned onion futures; no other crop is banned; it is still the law.** 7 U.S.C. § 13-1(a): "No contract
    for the sale of motion picture box office receipts ... or onions for future delivery shall be made on or subject to the
    rules of any board of trade in the United States." Source credit: Pub. L. 85–839, Aug. 28, 1958, 72 Stat. 1013; amended
    2010 (Pub. L. 111–203) to add box-office receipts. Penalty (b): a misdemeanour, "fined not more than $5,000".
    https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/7/13-1 · https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-2023-title7/html/USCODE-2023-title7-chap1-sec13-1.htm
    CEA decision p. 15: "Trading in onion futures has been prohibited by Act of Congress (Public Law 85-839 ... August 28, 1958".
    NPR: "It's the only agricultural product that it is illegal to trade futures in."
    → Script: "In 1958, Congress banned onion futures. No other crop. Still the law." Tag: "1958". Receipt: a capture of the
    Cornell LII page of § 13-1. "Garlic's legal." (the statute names onions only; a joke line, not advice)

  threat. The script says only what Finding 7 says.
