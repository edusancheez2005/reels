In twenty twenty-one, an anonymous hacker stole six hundred and ten million dollars in crypto. Then they gave it back.

The victim: Poly Network, a bridge that moves coins between blockchains. A few computers called keepers approve every move, each with a secret key.

Here's the hole: any outside message could replace the keepers. The hacker swapped in their own key and took the coins.

But blockchain moves are public, so everyone could watch the loot. Tether froze thirty-three million of its dollar coins. Security firm SlowMist said it found the hacker's email.

The next day, the hacker started sending coins back, writing on the blockchain: "I am not very interested in money." Poly nicknamed the hacker "Mr. White Hat" and offered a five-hundred-thousand-dollar reward. Thirteen days after the hack, the last coins came back.

Poly's mistake: anyone could change who held the keys. The fix: only the keepers should change the keepers, because whoever picks them controls every coin.
