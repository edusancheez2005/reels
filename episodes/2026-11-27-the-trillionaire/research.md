## Facts (each with its source)
1. The note: the Reserve Bank of Zimbabwe introduced a Z$100 trillion note on 16 January 2009, with 10, 20 and 50 trillion notes. C: "The Reserve Bank of Zimbabwe (RBZ) has introduced a new family of trillion Zim-dollar banknotes in denominations of 100 trillion, 50 trillion, 20 trillion and 10 trillion". B: "The Reserve Bank of Zimbabwe is introducing three other notes in trillion-dollar denominations of 10, 20 and 50." O: "introduced the $100 trillion banknote on 16 January 2009". (The note is printed with the year 2008, which is why G and the Reserve Bank call it a 2008 note.) Script: "Zimbabwe, January 2009. One banknote."
2. Fourteen zeros: F: "One hundred trillion, by the way, is a 1 with 14 zeroes". B prints "100,000,000,000,000"; G's record of the note's face: "100 000 000 000 000". Script: "Fourteen zeros." On screen: 100,000,000,000,000.
   Not claimed: "the biggest note ever" (O and G say "one of the world's largest"; Hungary's 1946 pengő notes were bigger).
3. Worth on day one: A: "worth about $US33 on the black market"; O: "BBC News reporting that the $100 trillion note was worth around US$30 or £20 on its first day in circulation". Script: "Day one: about thirty American dollars' worth."
   Disagreement, noted: B (AFP) says "about 300 US dollars (225 euros) at Thursdays exchange rate on the informal market" (the day before). Two sources give about $30 for the day itself, so the script says "about thirty".
4. What it bought soon after: E (reporting the Guardian): "Back in 2009, as massive hyperinflation in the impoverished African country hit a peak, one of the massive notes would have barely bought a bus fare." P: "trillion dollar notes which would not buy a bus ticket in Zimbabwe seven years ago". O: "a $100 trillion banknote could not pay for a simple bus fare". R: "It was worth as little as US$5 and could only buy two loaves of bread." Script (hook): "it barely covers the bus fare"; "Soon: one bus fare." (no source dates it more closely than "back in 2009"; the note was issued 16 Jan and the currency was suspended 12 April 2009, fact 8.) "The next note down is fifty trillion." is fact 1.
   The plan's "one loaf of bread" is not used: only R gives bread (two loaves), the Guardian line gives the bus fare.
5. Everyone was a trillionaire: O (Jan 2009): salaries "in the trillions per month" amounted "to around US$1"; Q (July 2008): salaries raised "to an average of two trillion Zimbabwean dollars". "You are the richest person on this bus. So is everyone else on this bus." is the joke built on that; it states no figure.
6. How: money printing. O: "this hyperinflation was linked to the Reserve Bank of Zimbabwe increasing the money supply"; "The Reserve Bank printed Z$21 trillion dollars to pay off debts owed to the International Monetary Fund." J (Hanke): the Reserve Bank supplied "the government with credit (read: print money)". Script: "The central bank kept printing money. More notes, same bread. Prices rose, so it printed bigger notes." Bigger notes: Q: a 100 billion-dollar note in July 2008 "which was not enough to buy a loaf of bread", then the trillion series in January 2009 (fact 1).
7. How fast: I (Hanke): "On November 14 2008, Zimbabwe's hyperinflation peaked at a year-over-year rate of 89.7 sextillion per cent." "At that rate, prices were doubling every 24.7 hours." It is one economist's estimate (the last official figure was 231 million percent for July 2008: H, "annual inflation raced to a record 231-million percent in July", Central Statistical Office). Script: "Two months earlier, at the worst point, prices doubled about every day. One economist's estimate." (14 Nov 2008 to 16 Jan 2009 = two months.) The 231 million and the sextillion figure are left out of the voice (one number).
8. The end of the currency: K: state media quoted the Economic Planning Minister saying the Zimbabwean dollar "will be out for at least a year"; headline "Zimbabwe Suspends Use of Own Currency" (12 April 2009). L (citing the BBC, 12 April 2009): Zimbabwe "has suspended the use of the Zimbabwe dollar for at least a year." Foreign currencies had been legal since January (K, L). Script: "April 2009. Zimbabwe stops using its own dollar. In the shops, your fortune buys: nothing." (the currency was suspended; the note could still be swapped later, fact 9)
9. The swap, 2015: M: the Reserve Bank's demonetisation "would begin on 15 June, and is expected to be completed by 30 September"; "an exchange rate of Z$250 trillion to US$1 for the 2008 note series". N: "On the notes issued in 2008, 40c will be paid for ZW$ 100,000,000,000,000 (ZW$ 100 trillion)." D (Reuters): "the 40 U.S. cents on offer from the central bank". Sum: 100 trillion / 250 trillion = US$0.40. Script: "Six years on, the central bank will swap your note. (2009 to 2015) For forty American cents."
10. Collectors: D (Reuters, 12 June 2015): "On online auction site eBay, a 100 trillion Zimbabwean dollar note is a collector's item, fetching up to $35", "a small fortune compared with the 40 U.S. cents on offer from the central bank". E (May 2016): "single 100 trillion-dollar bills are fetching up to $57 on auction site Ebay, the Guardian said". Script: "That year, on eBay, collectors paid up to thirty-five dollars for one." (a dated past price, June 2015; nothing about what one fetches now or will fetch).
11. The receipt on screen: source D's page (Reuters via VOA, 12 June 2015), captured 27 Nov 2026.
