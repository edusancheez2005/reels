In December 2018, a crypto boss died, and seventy-six thousand customers were owed two hundred and fifteen million Canadian dollars.
Gerald Cotten, thirty, ran QuadrigaCX, an exchange holding people's coins.
His widow said the coins were offline on his laptop, and she didn't know the password.
Everyone thought the money died with him.
The exchange collapsed. A court sent Ernst and Young's accountants to find the coins.
But Quadriga's six offline wallets had been empty since April, eight months before he died.
Where did the money go? Ontario's regulator found he'd made fake accounts with fake balances.
He traded that fake money with real customers, then covered his losses with other customers' deposits.
Two things let him do it: nobody checked his books, and customers couldn't see their coins.
A balance on an exchange is a promise, not proof. Keep savings in your own wallet, and write its backup words on paper, so only you can move your coins.
