## Facts used (every on-screen and spoken claim)

1. **1720, and who he was.** Britannica: "born Jan. 4, 1643, Woolsthorpe, Lincolnshire, Eng.—died March 31, 1727, London", so
   he was 77 through 1720 (PDF sec. 3: "In 1720, Newton was almost 80."). Britannica: "the theory of universal gravitation";
   "Principia Mathematica (1687)"; "He worked out the fundamentals of calculus"; "it is now well established that Newton
   developed calculus first, but that Leibniz was the first to publish".
   → Script: "It's 1720." "You explained gravity." / "[suit] And calculus." Tag: "London, 1720". (His age is not said.)
   "The cleverest man in England" is the narrator's flattery of "you", not a measured fact; it is not on screen.
2. **He ran the Royal Mint.** PDF sec. 3: "From 1699 to the end of his life in 1727 Newton was Master of the Mint" (Warden
   from 1696). PT: "In 1699 he became master of the Mint."
   → Script: "You work at the Royal Mint." / "[suit] I run it." / "You run it."
3. **Nearly everything he owned went into one stock.** PT: "he moved essentially all his financial assets into it, and he did so
   rapidly with a series of investments in mid-June." PDF sec. 4: at the end of 1720 "essentially all his wealth was in South
   Sea securities". (At the start of 1720 "slightly less than half of Newton's assets were in South Sea Stock", PDF sec. 3.)
   → Script (hook): "you're about to bet nearly everything on one stock" ("bet" is the narrator's word for an investment that lost); "With nearly everything."
4. **The South Sea Company, and the April sale at a profit.** Oxford: "In 1711, English speculators formed the South Sea Company
   in order to exploit a government-granted monopoly trade with Spain's Latin American colonies"; PT: "the company would take
   over most of the British national debt in 1720". PDF sec. 5: he chose to "liquidate his entire, or almost entire, stake ...
   in the space of less than one week, on Tuesday and Saturday, 19 and 23 April" (30 shares, then 70). PDF sec. 4: "he made a
   profit of about £20,000 in the early stages of the Bubble." Smithsonian (quoting Jason Zweig) gives a smaller figure, a
   "100% profit totalling £7000"; the sources disagree on the size, so the script gives no figure.
   → Script: "You own the hot stock: the South Sea Company. In April you sell, at a huge profit." (he already held it: PDF sec. 2, "Newton was an early investor"; fact 3)
5. **It kept rising after he sold.** Oxford: "nearly £600 by the end of May, and £950 by the end of June." PT: "The price of
   South Sea stock soared through the summer of 1720".
   → Script: "It keeps rising. Without you." (he held little or none between 23 April and mid-June: fact 4 and fact 6)
   "People buy because it's rising. It's rising because people buy. That's a bubble." is the narrator's one-line definition of
   a bubble (Oxford: the company "began to act almost exclusively as a kind of bank, lending money to potential purchasers of
   its stock"; PT's deck: "groupthink led him to plunge into the South Sea Bubble"). It is a definition, not a claim about why
   Newton bought.
6. **June: he bought back in, almost at the top.** PT: "Newton jumped back in almost at the peak." PDF: "and jumped back in,
   almost at the very top"; "with most of his investments in mid-June"; "by that time prices were much higher, and close to
   their peak"; "he continued to pour money into the South Sea Stock even as its price was beginning to slide, before the
   precipitous collapse in September 1720."
   → Script: "June. Near the top, you buy back in."
7. **The price: a peak of £1,050, and £124 in December 1720.** Oxford: "The overinflated share prices peaked at £1050"; it
   "suddenly dropped to £190 (below their original value) in the late summer, and even further to £124 in December 1720."
   Smithsonian: "Between January and June of that year, the shares in the South Sea Company skyrocketed from £128 each to
   £1050." "By September, they were again worth £175." PT: "By October, the stock was worth less than a quarter of its peak
   price." (PDF: "around 130" at the start of 1720.)
   → Script: "It peaks at a thousand and fifty pounds. By December: ... A hundred and twenty-four." Tags: "£1,050 a share",
   "£124". The wall chart in the study is a diagram drawn through these sourced points only (start of 1720: £128; end of
   May: nearly £600; end of June: £950; peak: £1,050; September: £175–190; December: £124), straight lines between them.
   The script does not say he paid £1,050; it says he bought "near the top" (fact 6).
8. **What it cost him: about a third of his fortune (a historian's count); legend says more.** PDF sec. 4: his net worth before
   the bubble "was somewhat over £30,000" (PT: "just over £30 000"); "By mid-1721, his net worth was down to about £20,000.
   This represents a loss of about £10,000." 10,000 ÷ 30,000 ≈ a third (my arithmetic). "A famous anecdote claims he lost
   twice that much. That is plausible, since there are various ways to count one's investment losses." Oxford: "Famously,
   Newton himself supposedly lost a fortune of around £20,000"; "It is likely that Newton lost some money, but the evidence is
   inconclusive." So the figure is disputed, as the plan warned.
   → Script: "One study reckons it cost him about a third of his fortune." (attributed to the study: Odlyzko is a mathematician
   at the University of Minnesota who writes financial history, so the voice says "one study", not "a historian"; no pound
   figure in the voice.)
   The caption gives both: about £10,000 by Odlyzko's count, £20,000 in the famous version.
9. **The line about the madness of people is attributed, not documented.** PT: "Newton allegedly said that he could 'calculate
   the motions of the heavenly bodies, but not the madness of people.'" PDF: "supposedly". Smithsonian: "One quotation
   attributed to him from this period".
   → Script: "He supposedly said he could calculate the heavens, but not the madness of people." ("the heavens" shortens "the motions of the heavenly bodies"; it is reported speech, hedged, never shown as a quotation)
   Never shown as a quote card: only the documented letter (fact 10) is.
10. **His own documented words, four years later.** Letter from Newton to the mathematician Nicolas Fatio de Duillier, 14
   September 1724, as quoted by PT and PDF: "I lost very much by the South Sea company which makes my pockets empty, & my mind
   averse from dealing in these matters." (Odlyzko calls "pockets empty" an exaggeration: fact 11.)
   → Caption only, in his exact words. Not on screen (no scene had room for it without fighting the voice).
11. **He was not ruined.** PT: the pre-bubble net worth "is also the approximate value of his estate at his death in 1727, so
   the bubble hardly ruined him financially." → Caption only ("he died a wealthy man").
12. **This was Isaac Newton.** Receipt: the Physics Today article's own headline and deck, captured from the live page:
   "Isaac Newton and the perils of the financial South Sea" / "Despite Newton's general brilliance and his expertise in
   finance, groupthink led him to plunge into the South Sea Bubble and lose much of his fortune." Tag: "Isaac Newton".
   → Script: "This was Isaac Newton." The one thing to remember is the attributed line itself (fact 9): being able to
   calculate the heavens did not help with a bubble. PT's deck says the same in its own words. No advice, no prediction.

## Not used, and why
- "He forbade anyone to say 'South Sea' in his presence" (Smithsonian, via Zweig): PDF sec. 6 says it "has only been traced to
  a period about two generations after Newton's death". Cut.
- A pound figure for the profit (£20,000 in PDF, £7,000 in Zweig): the sources disagree. Cut.
- The apple: a legend. An apple sits on the desk as a prop, and in the last shot one drops on the cartoon suit guy's head
  when the narrator finally says "Gravity." That is the cartoon's gag (the caption says the characters are made up); nothing
  is said or shown about an apple falling on Newton.
- The Hall estate trades (Newton was one of four executors): not his own money; left out to keep the story to his.
- The share price is the price of £100 of stock; "a thousand and fifty pounds" is how Oxford and Smithsonian give it.

## The fiction (says so in the caption)
The suit guy is "you", a cartoon, not a likeness of Newton: no portrait, no wig on him. His lines ("And calculus.", "I run
it.", "Don't.", "Back to gravity?") and Stonks the broker ("Nothing comes down.") are jokes, never stated as things Newton or
anyone real said or felt. "Fine. Gravity." is the narrator's.
