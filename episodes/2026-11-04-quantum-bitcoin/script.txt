Google says a big enough quantum computer could crack a Bitcoin key in minutes. Six million bitcoin are exposed.

That machine doesn't exist yet. Google's Willow chip has a hundred and five qubits, its building blocks. Cracking a key would need under half a million qubits. Nobody knows when.

Here's what it would break. Your bitcoin sits behind a lock, called a public key. Only your private key, a secret number, opens it. Today's computers can't work out the secret from the lock. A quantum one could.

But it has to see the lock first. An address, where you receive bitcoin, usually covers the lock until you spend. The exceptions: Bitcoin's earliest coins, some newer address types, and addresses reused after spending. That's the six million, says data firm Glassnode: nearly a third of all bitcoin.

A first step, still a draft: B-I-P three-sixty, a version of those newer addresses that keeps the lock covered.

Not everyone's worried. Crypto firm CoinShares says only about ten thousand of the earliest bitcoin face real risk.

What you can do today: don't reuse an address. Let your wallet give you a fresh one, so your lock stays covered.
